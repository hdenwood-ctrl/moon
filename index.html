Space travel
Space Environment

How warm is it when travelling in space?

Difficulty: Easy
Space has no air, so there is no normal “air temperature” like on Earth. In direct sunlight, spacecraft surfaces can heat to about 120°C, while shaded areas can drop below –150°C. Life‑support systems keep the inside of the spacecraft at a safe, comfortable temperature for astronauts.
Rocket Performance

How fast does a rocket travel?

Difficulty: Medium
Rockets must reach about 28,000 km/h to stay in orbit around Earth. On the way to the Moon, spacecraft can reach around 39,000 km/h. The speed changes as fuel burns and as the spacecraft moves away from Earth’s gravity.
Spacecraft Control

How do you turn a rocket?

Difficulty: Medium
Rockets turn using small side‑thrusters and engines that can swivel, called gimballed engines. In space there is almost no air resistance, so even tiny pushes can slowly rotate a spacecraft. Onboard computers constantly adjust these thrusters to keep the rocket stable and on course.
Mission Risks

What is the biggest problem for rockets in space?

Difficulty: Advanced
Micrometeoroids and space debris can hit spacecraft at very high speeds and cause serious damage. Engine or system failures can leave a mission unable to manoeuvre or complete its goals. Radiation and extreme temperatures slowly wear down electronics and materials over long missions.
Collision Scenario

What would happen if you crashed into an asteroid?

Difficulty: Medium
A spacecraft hitting an asteroid at high speed would be destroyed almost instantly. The impact would release a huge amount of energy because of the high relative speed. Mission planners carefully track asteroid positions and design flight paths to avoid collisions.
Mission Duration

How long does it take to get to the Moon?

Difficulty: Easy
The Apollo missions took about 3 days to travel from Earth to the Moon. Modern crewed missions would take a similar amount of time. The average distance to the Moon is about 384,000 km.
Human Cost

How many people have died in space?

Difficulty: Medium
No one has died in the vacuum of outer space itself. However, astronauts and cosmonauts have died during launches, re‑entries, and training accidents. Major tragedies include Apollo 1, Challenger, and Columbia, which led to big safety improvements.
Moon environment
Lunar Environment

How warm is it on the Moon?

Difficulty: Easy
The Moon has almost no atmosphere, so temperatures change a lot between day and night. In the daytime, the surface can reach about 127°C. At night, it can drop to around –173°C, so astronauts need very protective spacesuits.
Lunar Gravity

How high do you jump on the Moon?

Difficulty: Easy
The Moon’s gravity is about one‑sixth as strong as Earth’s. A 0.5 m jump on Earth could be around 3 m on the Moon in theory. Apollo astronauts didn’t jump that high because their suits were heavy and they had to stay safe.
Lunar Cycle

How long is a day/night on the Moon?

Difficulty: Medium
One full lunar day, from sunrise to sunrise at the same spot, lasts about 27 Earth days. That means roughly 13.5 days of daylight followed by 13.5 days of darkness. This long day‑night cycle helps cause the Moon’s extreme temperature changes.
Human Experience

What is walking on the Moon like?

Difficulty: Easy
Walking on the Moon feels light and bouncy because of the lower gravity. Astronauts often used a “bunny‑hop” style to stay balanced and move efficiently. The lunar dust is fine and powdery, but it can be slippery and sticks to suits and equipment.
History & origin
Exploration History

Why did everyone stop being interested in the Moon?

Difficulty: Medium
After the Apollo landings, many people felt the main goal—reaching the Moon—had already been achieved. Lunar missions were very expensive, and governments moved money to other priorities. Interest is rising again now because of new technology and plans for future Moon bases.
Moon Formation

How did the Moon come into existence?

Difficulty: Advanced
The leading idea is the Giant Impact Hypothesis. It says that a Mars‑sized object hit the young Earth about 4.4 billion years ago. Debris from the collision formed a ring around Earth, which slowly clumped together to become the Moon.
Future Exploration

Who was the first woman on the Moon?

Difficulty: Easy
So far, no woman has walked on the Moon. All 12 Moon‑walkers from the Apollo missions were men. NASA’s Artemis program plans to land the first woman and the first person of colour on the Moon soon.
Bonus facts
Earth Connection

How does the Moon affect Earth’s tides?

Difficulty: Easy
The Moon’s gravity pulls on Earth’s oceans, creating high and low tides. When the Moon, Earth, and Sun line up, tides are stronger and called spring tides. Without the Moon, Earth’s tides would be much weaker and coastal life would be very different.
Lunar Surface

Why does the Moon have so many craters?

Difficulty: Medium
The Moon has no thick atmosphere to burn up incoming rocks. Asteroids and meteoroids hit the surface and leave impact craters that last for millions or billions of years. There is almost no weather or erosion on the Moon, so old craters never really fade away.

Secret fact 1: Why do we always see the same side of the Moon?

Difficulty: Advanced
KEYCODE UNLOCK
The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, which means it rotates once on its axis in the same time it takes to orbit Earth. Because of this, the same side of the Moon always faces us, and the far side is never seen from Earth’s surface. Spacecraft had to orbit the Moon to photograph and map the far side for the first time.

Secret fact 2: Why doesn’t the Moon fall into Earth?

Difficulty: Medium
KEYCODE UNLOCK
Earth’s gravity pulls the Moon inward, but the Moon is also moving sideways very fast. This sideways motion means it keeps “falling around” Earth instead of straight down. The balance between gravity and motion keeps the Moon in orbit.

Secret fact 3: Will the Moon always stay the same distance away?

Difficulty: Advanced
KEYCODE UNLOCK
The Moon is slowly moving away from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year. This happens because tidal forces transfer energy from Earth’s rotation to the Moon’s orbit. In the very distant future, eclipses will look different because the Moon will appear smaller in our sky.
